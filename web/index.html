Delay to the lights

Counted from the moment a Sync Box frame reaches the board. Not included: the box's own smoothing and the Wi-Fi hop from the box to the board (see docs/LATENCY.md). The board's share is measured; the network share is half of a probe's round trip and the strip refresh is an estimate, so this is not a measurement of the light itself. A Govee light takes at most about 12 frames a second, so a new frame can wait up to 80 ms for its turn.

The Sync Box is sending, but its data cannot be read

Data is arriving on the Sync Box port, but none of it can be decoded, so the lights are not following the picture. This usually means the box's firmware changed what it sends.

Press Download diagnostics and attach the file to an issue on the project's GitHub page, together with your box's firmware versions (from its app). The file holds counters and a few raw frames (colours and sizes only: no addresses). If you kept a capture from before the update, tools/check_capture.py can compare it with the new one (see docs/RESILIENCE.md).

1. Connect to your Wi-Fi

The board restarts and joins your network. Then open http://fancy2wled.local (or the address your router shows) from a device on the same network. It must be on the same network/VLAN as the Sync Box.

Targets

A target is a WLED strip or a Govee light that follows one part of the picture, for example a strip behind the TV and a cabinet light. The sections below edit the target you pick here. The Look settings are shared; each target has its own brightness.

Smooth blends neighbouring zones along the strip. Sharp gives each zone its own block with a hard edge, like the Sync Box's own strip.

Blends between two zones along the colour wheel (red, orange, yellow, green) instead of mixing them in RGB, which turns unlike colours into grey or brown in between. Best with smooth edges on a long strip.

Softens the steps between zones: each LED is averaged with its neighbours over about this many LEDs. A little (4 to 6 LEDs) turns blocks into a soft blob that slides along the strip; more is blurrier. It only touches the part of the strip along the picture, and costs about 1 % of the board's processing when used on a few hundred LEDs.

Picture map

The 24 zones of the Sync Box, drawn where each one really looks at the picture (the box averages those areas and never sends the parts in between), and which of them each target follows. The box only drives the edges, so the middle zones are never used. Tap a zone for its colour, or a target's bar for how many of its LEDs follow it. The map follows your changes before you save them.

WLED

A Govee light must have LAN control switched on in the Govee Home app (the light's settings). The board sends it a few colours per second and the light stretches them evenly over its whole length. The light is addressed by its IP address.

2. The whole run: how long is it?

The run is the stretch of your strip you want the TV to control; the rest of the strip is left alone. For example, if the first 210 LEDs of the strip are behind and beside the TV: first LED 0, length 210.

3. Which part of the run covers the TV?

Give the strip's own LED numbers where the TV starts and ends (). Along that part the colours follow the picture exactly; the LEDs before and after it fade smoothly (see Smooth beyond the TV below). Leave both empty if the whole run is exactly as long as the TV.

The picture is spread over the part of the strip that runs along the screen's edge. Give the first and last segment of that part (segment 0 is where the light starts); the segments outside it hold the colour of the zone at that end, or stay dark if you tick the box below. If the whole strip runs along the edge, leave both empty. Find my segments below shows where each segment starts and ends on the strip.

When you change these numbers, red LEDs light up on the strip where the TV starts and ends, and blue ones where the run starts and ends. The middle button adds a green LED at the exact middle of the TV part, to check that it sits in the middle of the screen.

Look

Motion smoothing slides the lights from one Sync Box picture to the next instead of jumping, so movement looks continuous. The box sends a new picture only about every 100 ms; at 100 the lights run one picture behind (about 100 ms) so they always have the next one to slide towards, at lower values less. While it is on it replaces Smoothing over time and Reaction boost. 0 is off.

The Sync Box smooths its own colours, which is most of the delay between the picture and the lights. In the box's app, Speed of Diffusion sets how far each packet moves towards the new picture (40% = 40% of the way, so a change takes about half a second to arrive). The best fix is to set the box's speed to 100%: it then sends the real picture at once. Set Box speed here to the same number as in the box's app. Reaction boost undoes the smoothing in software when the box is slower than 100%: 0 follows the box exactly, 100 rebuilds the picture it is aiming at (it does nothing at a box speed of 100). A lost packet at the wrong moment can make one frame overshoot, so around 60 is a good start. At high values fast flashes look sharper and a dark scene can flick to black a little early.

LEDs along the TV follow the picture exactly. LEDs past the edge of the TV show the colour of the corner zone at their end of the strip (for LEDs on the ceiling: the top-left zone at the left end, the top-right zone at the right end), reaching it at the end of the strip. 100% = it takes the whole strip, lower = it blends faster, 0 = the end LEDs just repeat the TV's edge colour.

When the TV is off

The Sync Box sends no signal of its own when the TV is switched off (checked with the TV's power button, over CEC): it keeps sending, with a completely black picture. So the board decides the TV is off when the picture has been completely black for a while, and on again with the first colour. A long black screen counts as off too, which is why the delay is a setting.

Home Assistant (MQTT)

The board connects to your MQTT broker (for example Home Assistant's Mosquitto add-on) and appears in Home Assistant by itself as a Fancy2WLED device: a Sync light (on/off and brightness), status sensors and the Look settings. The board connects out to the broker, so your firewall must let it reach the broker (TCP, usually port 1883).

Off by default. When on, the board measures its CPU load, memory and frame timing and publishes them every 5 seconds to <topic prefix>/perf, plus a set of diagnostic sensors in Home Assistant. The measuring itself uses a little CPU, so switch it off when you are done: it then stops measuring and publishing, and the sensors disappear from Home Assistant. Needs MQTT to be connected.

Firmware updates

The last update did not start properly, so the board went back to the previous firmware.

The firmware that just started is being checked for about a minute. A new update is accepted after that.

Updates over Wi-Fi are off until you set an update password and switch them on. The password is what protects the board: anyone who knows it and can reach this page can replace the firmware. Use one you do not use anywhere else (it travels unencrypted on your network, like the rest of this page).

Set an update password above to be able to switch updates on.

The file is firmware.bin from .pio/build/atoms3 (or the one attached to a release). Settings are kept. The WLEDs are handed back while it installs and the board restarts by itself; if the new firmware does not start properly the board returns to the old one.

From a computer with PlatformIO: set the environment variable FANCY2WLED_OTA_PASSWORD to the update password, then run pio run -e atoms3-ota -t upload.

Back up / move to another board

Export saves your WLED address, LED layout, phone calibration, Look settings and MQTT settings to a file. Import loads such a file onto this board, for example a new ESP32 after you have set up its Wi-Fi. The Wi-Fi name and password are never included (a new board joins your Wi-Fi first, then you import).
